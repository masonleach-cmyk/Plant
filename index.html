    Primer: ODEs and PDEs

    A ground-up refresher on the math under everything else in this app. No prior memory of the course assumed. Every step has a graph you can push around.

    The plan

    The Richards equation is a partial differential equation (PDE): it describes how the water content at every depth in a pot changes over time. To read it comfortably you need two ideas.

    ODE, ordinary differential equation: one quantity changing over time. The total water in a pot, W(t).
    PDE, partial differential equation: a quantity changing over time and space. The water content at each depth, θ(z, t).

    Part 1 builds up ODEs from the idea of a slope. Part 2 extends the same ideas to PDEs. Part 3 gives θ its real units, adds the second variable h, gravity and roots, and ends with the Richards equation read term by term. The primer uses the Richards equation's own letters throughout: θ for water content, q for flux, z for depth (measured down), K for conductivity, h for pressure head, S for roots. Work through in order; each step uses the one before. Every step ends with a key idea and a check yourself question: answer it in your head, then tap to compare. The hardest ideas have an Explain it more slowly box that walks through them with numbers, and Try it in the Primer lab buttons open the same equation in the Primer lab tab so you can change it yourself. Seven quizzes, marked Q in the list below, check your understanding as you go. Steps with a letter, like 7a, slow down the parts of Part 2 that need more room.

    Part 1: change over time (ODEs)

    1. A derivative is a slope

    Imagine weighing a pot every hour. Call the water in it W(t), in mm, at time t in hours. The derivative dW/dt is how fast W is changing at one instant, in mm per hour. On a graph of W against t, it is the slope of the curve.

    You can't measure a slope at a single point, so start with two. The secant slope between t and t + Δt is rise over run:

    secant slope = ΔW/Δt
    = [W(t + Δt) − W(t)] / Δt

    Shrink Δt and the secant line rotates into the tangent line, which just touches the curve. The derivative is the slope of that tangent: the limit of ΔW/Δt as Δt → 0.

    Explain it more slowly

    Think of a car trip. Distance over total time gives the average speed for the trip; the speedometer gives the speed at one instant. ΔW/Δt is the trip average, dW/dt is the speedometer.

    With numbers: say W(2 h) = 50 mm and W(3 h) = 53 mm. The secant slope over that hour is 3 mm/h. Shrink the window: if W(2.1 h) = 50.4 mm, the slope over 0.1 h is 4 mm/h. Keep shrinking and the answer stops changing; that settled value is the derivative at t = 2 h.

    The units always come from the division: mm divided by hours is mm/h. That's a good habit for every derivative in this primer: ∂θ/∂z is "θ per cm", ∂θ/∂t is "θ per hour".

    Measure the tangent slope at every time and plot it. The derivative is a new function of t:

    Notice: drag Δt toward 0.1 h and the secant slope closes in on the tangent slope. Where the curve is momentarily flat (a peak or a dip of the daily wiggle), the derivative crosses zero. Where the curve falls, the derivative is negative.

    Key idea: dW/dt is the rate of change right now: the slope of W against t, in mm per hour. Positive means filling, negative means draining, zero means momentarily level.

    Check yourself: W is 40 mm at 8:00 and 37 mm at 11:00. What is the average dW/dt?

    (37 − 40) / 3 = −1 mm/h: draining at 1 mm per hour on average. At any single moment in between, the rate could be faster or slower.

    Check yourself: At the top of a peak in W(t), what is dW/dt?

    Zero. Just before the peak W is rising (positive slope), just after it is falling (negative), so the slope passes through zero at the top.

    2. A differential equation is a rule for the slope

    Usually you don't know W(t). What you know is a rule for how fast it changes, given how much is there. Say watering adds water at a steady rate q, in mm/h, and the pot loses water in proportion to what it holds. (q is a flux, a rate of water flow; the Richards equation uses the same letter.)

    dW/dt = q − W/τ

    This doesn't say what W is at noon. It says: whatever W is, here is its slope. The short lines below draw that rule at every time and water level. A solution is any curve that follows the lines everywhere. Each one needs a starting point, the initial condition.

    Explain it more slowly

    Read the equation as a sentence: "the rate of change of W equals the watering rate minus a loss proportional to W." Then plug in numbers instead of solving anything. With q = 5 mm/h and τ = 10 h:

    W = 80 dW/dt = 5 − 80/10 = −3 mm/h: draining
    W = 20 dW/dt = 5 − 20/10 = +3 mm/h: filling
    W = 50 dW/dt = 5 − 50/10 = 0: balanced

    So without solving anything you already know the story: above 50 mm the pot drains, below it the pot fills, so every pot heads for 50. That is all an equilibrium is: the W where the rule says "no change".

    Why do you need a starting point? The rule only hands out slopes. Two pots following the same rule but starting at different levels trace different curves, just as two cars obeying the same speed limit end up in different places if they start in different towns.

    Tap the chart to start a pot at that time and water level.

    Notice: every curve heads for the dashed line, W* = q·τ, where inflow equals outflow and the slope is zero. That is an equilibrium. The lines are the same at every time (the rule doesn't mention t), so each solution is the same shape shifted sideways.

    Key idea: A differential equation doesn't tell you W. It tells you how fast W changes for any W you plug in. Equilibria are the values of W where that rate is zero.

    Check yourself: With q = 2 mm/h and τ = 20 h, where does the pot settle? Is a pot at 60 mm filling or draining?

    It settles at W* = q·τ = 40 mm. At 60 mm the rule gives dW/dt = 2 − 60/20 = −1 mm/h, so it is draining toward 40.

    3. Solving it by taking steps

    A computer solves an ODE by literally following the slope field. Stand at the current point, read the slope the rule gives, walk along it for a time Δt, and repeat. This is Euler's method:

    W[k+1] = W[k] + Δt · f(W[k])
    f(W) = q − W/τ

    Here q = 1 mm/h, τ = 10 h, and the pot starts at 80 mm. The dashed arrow shows the step you're about to take.

    Explain it more slowly

    Two steps by hand, with dW/dt = 1 − W/10, W₀ = 80 mm and Δt = 2 h:

    step 1 rate = 1 − 80/10 = −7 mm/h; change = 2 × (−7) = −14; W = 66 mm
    step 2 rate = 1 − 66/10 = −5.6 mm/h; change = 2 × (−5.6) = −11.2; W = 54.8 mm

    Rate × time = change, over and over. The error comes from using the slope at the start of each step for the whole step: by the end of step 1 the real slope had already eased to −5.6, but Euler kept going at −7 the whole time, so it undershoots. Halve Δt and the error roughly halves.

    Notice: each step uses the slope at its start, but the true slope changes during the step. With small steps the error is tiny; with big ones Euler cuts well below the true curve, because it keeps using a slope that is steeper than the real one has become. Basics 2 shows what happens when the steps get too big.

    Key idea: Solving an ODE numerically means repeating one move: read the slope the rule gives, step forward Δt along it. Smaller steps follow the true curve more closely.

    Check yourself: W = 30 mm, dW/dt = 1 − W/10 and Δt = 0.5 h. What is W after one Euler step?

    The rate is 1 − 30/10 = −2 mm/h. One step changes W by Δt × rate = 0.5 × (−2) = −1 mm, so W becomes 29 mm.

    4. The exact solution and the time constant

    This particular ODE can be solved by hand. Write W∞ = q·τ for the equilibrium, so the rule is dW/dt = (W∞ − W)/τ. Then separate the variables and integrate:

    dW / (W − W∞) = −dt / τ
    ln|W − W∞| = −t/τ + constant
    W(t) = W∞ + (W₀ − W∞) · e^(−t/τ)

    The gap between W and its final value shrinks by the same fraction every hour. τ is the time constant: after one τ, 63% of the gap is closed. This is the step response of a first-order system or an RC circuit.

    Explain it more slowly

    The trick is to watch the gap instead of W. Call g = W − W∞, the distance still to go. Since W∞ is a constant, dg/dt = dW/dt = −g/τ: the gap shrinks at a rate proportional to itself.

    Only one kind of function does that: an exponential. Check it: if g = g₀·e^(−t/τ), its derivative is −(1/τ)·g₀·e^(−t/τ) = −g/τ. Adding W∞ back gives the formula above.

    after 1τ 37% of the gap left (63% closed)
    after 2τ 13.5% left
    after 3τ 5% left
    after 5τ under 1% left: "settled" for practical purposes

    In controls terms this is a first-order lag with time constant τ and steady-state value q·τ. Every linear system you have tuned behaves this way near its operating point.

    Notice: the dotted tangent at t = 0 reaches the final value exactly at t = τ. Change W₀ and the curve stretches vertically, but the timing marks don't move: in a linear ODE, how fast you settle doesn't depend on how far you have to go.

    Key idea: A linear first-order ODE always gives an exponential approach. τ, the time constant, is how long it takes to close 63% of the gap, whatever the size of the gap.

    Check yourself: τ = 10 h and the pot starts 30 mm below its equilibrium. How far below is it after 20 h?

    Twenty hours is two time constants, so the gap is multiplied by e^(−2) ≈ 0.135: about 30 × 0.135 ≈ 4 mm below.

    Check yourself: You want the pot to respond twice as fast without changing where it settles. What do you change?

    Halve τ and double q, so W* = q·τ stays the same. The Primer lab challenge "On target and on time" practices exactly this.

    5. Nonlinear ODEs: read the rate curve

    In soil, the outflow isn't proportional to W; conductivity makes it rise much more steeply. Take

    dW/dt = f(W) = q − k·W^p

    With p ≠ 1 the method from step 4 no longer gives a tidy formula. You can still learn a lot by plotting the rate f against the state W. Where f > 0, W grows (arrows point right); where f < 0, W shrinks. Where f crosses zero is an equilibrium.

    Explain it more slowly

    Take q = 4, k = 0.01 and p = 2, so dW/dt = 4 − 0.01·W². The equilibrium is where 0.01·W² = 4, so W* = 20 mm.

    Near W* the curve looks like a straight line with slope f′(W*) = −p·k·W*^(p−1) = −2 × 0.01 × 20 = −0.4 per hour. So close to 20 mm the pot behaves like step 4's linear pot with τ = 1/0.4 = 2.5 h. That is linearization: replace the curve by its tangent at the operating point.

    Far away the straight line is wrong. At W = 60 the real rate is 4 − 36 = −32 mm/h, while the tangent line predicts −0.4 × 40 = −16 mm/h. An overfull pot drains twice as fast as the linear model says. Soil does this too: K grows steeply with wetness, so wet soil drains much faster than a linear model predicts.

    Notice: near the equilibrium the curve is almost a straight line, so the pot behaves like the linear ODE from step 4 with a local time constant τ = 1/|f′(W*)|. That is linearization about an operating point. Far from it, the behavior is lopsided: with p > 1, an overfull pot drops fast while a dry one recovers slowly.

    Key idea: For a nonlinear ODE, read the rate curve f(W). Its zeros are equilibria, its sign gives the direction of motion, and its slope at a zero gives the local time constant.

    Check yourself: On a rate curve, an equilibrium where f goes from negative (to its left) to positive (to its right): stable or unstable?

    Unstable. Just to the right f > 0, so W grows and moves away; just to the left f < 0, so W shrinks and also moves away.

    6. Two tanks: a system of ODEs

    Split the pot into a top half and a bottom half holding W₁ and W₂ mm of water. Water flows between them in proportion to the difference, and the bottom also drains. Now there are two ODEs, and each one depends on the other:

    dW₁/dt = −g·(W₁ − W₂)
    dW₂/dt = g·(W₁ − W₂) − d·W₂

    In matrix form that is dW/dt = A·W. The state is a point (W₁, W₂), and the second chart, the phase plane, shows where it moves.

    Explain it more slowly

    Two different things can happen in two connected tanks:

    fast mode water sloshes through the pipe until the levels match. Its speed is set by the pipe g: τ ≈ 1/(2g).
    slow mode with the levels matched, both tanks drain together through the bottom. Its speed is set by the drain d: τ ≈ 2/d.

    With the default g = 0.5/h and d = 0.06/h that is about 1 h versus about 33 h. Any starting point is a mix of the two: first the difference between the tanks disappears, then the total slowly drains. An eigenvector is a starting shape that keeps its shape while it decays; its eigenvalue is the decay rate, 1/τ.

    Why care? A soil column cut into many layers is the same thing with many tanks. Thin layers have very fast modes and the whole pot has very slow ones, so the system is stiff. An explicit solver has to take tiny steps for the fast modes long after they have died out, which is why the lab uses an implicit method (Basics 2).

    Tap the phase plane to choose the starting levels.

    Notice: the solution is a mix of two modes, the eigenvectors of A (dashed lines). The fast mode is the tanks evening out; the slow mode is both draining together. A trajectory first runs parallel to the fast direction (red dashes, drawn through the start), then crawls in along the slow line toward empty. When the two time constants are far apart, the system is stiff.

    Key idea: Coupled ODEs are tanks talking to each other. The motion splits into modes, each with its own time constant. Very different time constants make the system stiff.

    Check yourself: Make the pipe g ten times bigger. Which time constant changes a lot?

    The fast one, the tanks evening out, gets about ten times shorter. The slow one, both tanks draining together, barely changes because it is set by the drain d.

    Part 2: change over time and space (PDEs)

    7. A function of two variables

    Cut the pot into more and more layers and you no longer have a list of tanks, you have a smooth function θ(z, t): the water content at depth z and time t, the same θ as in the Richards equation. In Part 2 θ runs on a simple scale from 0 (bone dry) to 1 (saturated), so the numbers stay easy; step 12 gives it real units. The heatmap shows it from above, with time across and depth down.

    A partial derivative is a slope taken while the other variable is held fixed. Slice across at one time to get the profile θ(z); its slope is ∂θ/∂z. Slice down at one depth to get the history θ(t); its slope is ∂θ/∂t.

    Explain it more slowly

    Picture two ways to measure the same pot. Freeze time and push a probe down through the soil: how fast θ changes per cm as you go is ∂θ/∂z, measured in "per cm". Or bury a sensor at one depth and log it all day: how fast its reading changes is ∂θ/∂t, in "per hour".

    The "partial" just means the other variable is held still. Everything you know about ordinary derivatives (slope, sign, units) carries over unchanged.

    The heat equation links the two: how fast a buried sensor's reading changes (∂θ/∂t) is set by how curved the profile is at that sensor's depth (∂²θ/∂z²). Steps 7c and 8 explain why.

    Tap the heatmap to pick a point.

    Notice: at the peak of the bump, the profile curves downward and θ is falling in time. Out on the flanks, the profile curves upward and θ is rising. Check the last two readouts at any point you pick: ∂θ/∂t always equals D·∂²θ/∂z². This function satisfies the heat equation, which is what it means to be a solution of a PDE.

    Key idea: A partial derivative is an ordinary derivative with blinkers on: ∂θ/∂z walks down the pot at one instant, ∂θ/∂t watches one depth over time.

    Check yourself: A moisture sensor at 10 cm shows its reading rising. Which partial derivative is positive, and where?

    ∂θ/∂t at z = 10 cm. The sensor stays at one depth, so it only sees change in time.

    Check yourself: θ at 6 cm is 0.30 at 2 h and 0.26 at 4 h. Estimate ∂θ/∂t at 3 h.

    7c. The second derivative: slope of the slope

    The heat equation turns on ∂²θ/∂z², so it's worth getting a feel for it. There are two equivalent ways to see it.

    slope of the slope take the slope ∂θ/∂z at every depth, plot it, then take its slope. Where the slope is increasing, the curve bends upward.
    compare with neighbors draw a straight line between the points a little above and a little below. If the curve sits below that line, it bends upward and ∂²θ/∂z² > 0. The gap is about (Δz²/2)·∂²θ/∂z².
    Explain it more slowly

    With numbers, 2 cm apart: θ(4 cm) = 0.20, θ(6 cm) = 0.30, θ(8 cm) = 0.20. The neighbors average 0.20, but the middle sits at 0.30: a hilltop.

    ∂²θ/∂z² ≈ (above − 2·here + below) / Δz² = (0.20 − 0.60 + 0.20) / 4 = −0.05 per cm². Negative, as a hilltop should be.

    The physical meaning: a layer that is wetter than the average of its neighbors gives water away to them, so it dries. A layer drier than its neighbors (a valley) gets water from both sides, so it wets. That is the heat equation in one sentence, and step 8 builds it properly.

    Notice: in a valley, the point sits below its neighbors' average, the gap is positive, and so is ∂²θ/∂z². On a hilltop it's the reverse. Shrink Δz and the last two readouts agree more and more closely. That neighbor comparison is exactly how the lab's solver computes curvature: (above − 2·this + below)/Δz².

    Key idea: ∂²θ/∂z² compares a point with the average of its neighbors: positive in valleys, negative on hilltops, zero on straight lines.

    Check yourself: θ above = 0.1, here = 0.2, below = 0.3: a straight ramp. What is ∂²θ/∂z²?

    Above − 2·here + below = 0.1 − 0.4 + 0.3 = 0. A straight line has no curvature, so under the heat equation it does not change at all.

    8. From shape to change

    Why should the curvature of a profile decide how fast it changes? Follow the chain one link at a time:

    slope ∂θ/∂z: how steeply θ changes with depth.
    flux q = −D·∂θ/∂z: water spreads downhill, from high θ to low, faster where it's steeper. D is the soil-water diffusivity: how readily water spreads. (Fourier for heat, Fick for solutes, Darcy for water.)
    balance ∂θ/∂t = −∂q/∂z: a thin layer gains what flows in its top minus what flows out its bottom.
    combine ∂θ/∂t = D·∂²θ/∂z²: the heat equation.
    Explain it more slowly: the signs

    z is measured downward, so q > 0 means water moving down. If the soil gets wetter as you go down, ∂θ/∂z is positive, and water should move up, toward the drier soil. Up is negative q. So q must have the opposite sign to the slope: q = −D·∂θ/∂z. The minus sign just says "downhill".

    Now take a thin layer from z to z + Δz. In a time Δt it gains q(z)·Δt through its top and loses q(z + Δz)·Δt through its bottom. Its water content changes by

    Δθ·Δz = [q(z) − q(z + Δz)]·Δt

    Divide by Δz and Δt and shrink both: ∂θ/∂t = −∂q/∂z. Substitute q = −D·∂θ/∂z and the two minus signs cancel: ∂θ/∂t = D·∂²θ/∂z².

    That is why the Ramp preset never changes: its slope is the same everywhere, so the flux is the same everywhere, so every layer passes on exactly what it receives.

    Drag the dots up and down to draw your own profile.

    Notice: pick Ramp. There is flux everywhere, but nothing changes, because each layer passes on exactly what it receives. Only where the flux changes along depth, at the bends, does θ rise or fall. Sharp bends mean fast change.

    Key idea: Flux comes from the slope, and change comes from how the flux varies with depth. Two derivatives in a row: that is where the ∂² in the heat equation comes from.

    Check yourself: 0.5 cm/h of water flows into a layer through its top and 0.3 cm/h flows out through its bottom. Is the layer getting wetter or drier?

    Wetter: it keeps 0.2 cm of water per hour. Divided by the layer thickness, that is its ∂θ/∂t. In = out would mean no change, however large the flow.

    8a. A layer budget you can count

    Here is step 8 with five layers and actual numbers. Water moves across each boundary in proportion to the difference between the two layers it separates:

    moved across a boundary in one step
    = r · (θ above − θ below)

    r bundles together D, the step length, and the layer thickness: r = D·Δt/Δz². Each layer then gains what comes in through its top boundary and loses what leaves through its bottom. The top and bottom of the column are sealed.

    Tap inside a layer's column to set its level.

    Notice: the total never changes, because whatever one layer loses through a boundary, its neighbor gains. Write out "in minus out" for a middle layer and it collapses to r·(above − 2·this + below): the same neighbor comparison as step 7c. That is why conservation plus downhill flow gives curvature. Now set r above 0.5 and step: the levels overshoot and seesaw, the explicit-step instability from Basics 2.

    Key idea: Conservation plus downhill flow gives the neighbor rule: each layer changes by r·(above − 2·this + below).

    Check yourself: With r = 0.2 and layers at 0.4, 0.6, 0.4, how much does the middle layer change in one step?

    0.2 × (0.4 − 1.2 + 0.4) = −0.08, so it drops to 0.52. Each neighbor gains 0.04, so the total is unchanged.

    9. Watch the heat equation run

    Start from the profile you drew in step 8 and let ∂θ/∂t = D·∂²θ/∂z² play forward. A PDE also needs boundary conditions: what happens at the top and bottom edges.

    Explain it more slowly: kinds of boundary
    sealed no flux through the edge (a "Neumann" condition, q = 0). A pot base with no hole, or a covered surface.
    held value θ (or h) is fixed at the edge (a "Dirichlet" condition). Standing water on the surface, or a water table at the base.
    given flux q is fixed at the edge. Rain, a drip line, or watering at a known rate.
    free drainage water leaves the bottom at rate K, pulled by gravity alone. The lab's default for pots with drain holes.

    The PDE describes the inside; the boundaries describe the outside world. The same PDE with different boundaries gives completely different answers, as the three buttons above show. In the Primer lab's PDE workbench you can mix and match them.

    Notice: with sealed ends, the total never changes; the profile just flattens toward its average. With the top held wet, water seeps in through the boundary and the total climbs. Sharp corners vanish first, because they have the most curvature.

    Key idea: A PDE needs boundary conditions as well as a starting profile. The interior rule alone does not know what the edges do.

    Check yourself: Which boundary condition fits a drip emitter delivering 2 mm/h onto the surface?

    A flux condition: q at the top is fixed at 0.2 cm/h, however wet the surface is.

    9a. How long does spreading take?

    Diffusion has a built-in clock. A bump of width σ₀ spreads so that its width grows like

    σ(t)² = σ₀² + 2·D·t

    Width squared grows in a straight line with time, so width itself grows only as √t. To spread twice as far takes four times as long. The rule of thumb is t ≈ L²/D: the time for diffusion to even things out over a distance L.

    Explain it more slowly: why √t

    Diffusion is the average of many random moves. Each bit of water takes small steps in random directions, as likely up as down. After n steps the typical distance from the start is about √n steps, not n, because most moves cancel out.

    So distance grows like √t, and time grows like distance squared. With D = 20 cm²/h, evening out over 10 cm takes about 100/20 = 5 h, but over 20 cm about 400/20 = 20 h. Doubling the pot's depth makes spreading alone four times slower, which is one reason gravity matters so much in deep pots.

    Notice: on the width-squared view the points fall on a straight line with slope 2D.

    Key idea: Diffusion distance grows like √(D·t), so the time to spread over a distance L grows like L²/D.

    Check yourself: Spreading over 5 cm takes 2 h. How long for 15 cm?

    Three times the distance takes 3² = 9 times as long: about 18 h.

    9b. Steady state: when nothing changes

    Hold the top and bottom at fixed values and wait. Eventually the profile stops changing: ∂θ/∂t = 0. That is a steady state, and setting the left side of the equation to zero turns the PDE into an ODE in z alone:

    0 = D·d²θ/dz² − v·dθ/dz

    With no drift, d²θ/dz² = 0 means zero curvature: a straight line between the two boundary values. With drift, the profile bends into an exponential that hugs one end.

    Notice: at steady state the flux is the same at every depth. It has to be: if any layer took in more than it passed on, it would be changing. While the profile is still settling, the flux varies with depth. In a pot, "full after draining" in the lab is a steady state with zero flux everywhere, where gravity and the pressure gradient cancel. In terms of h, that profile is a straight line: h = −(height above the base).

    Key idea: At steady state ∂θ/∂t = 0. The PDE becomes an ODE in z alone; with no drift the profile is a straight line and the flux is the same at every depth.

    Check yourself: Top held at 0.8, bottom at 0.2, D = 2 cm²/h, 20 cm deep, no drift. What is the steady flux?

    The slope is (0.2 − 0.8)/20 = −0.03 per cm, so q = −D·slope = −2 × (−0.03) = 0.06 cm/h, flowing down.

    10. Adding a drift: gravity

    Diffusion spreads things out in both directions. Gravity pulls water one way. A drift at speed v adds one term:

    ∂θ/∂t = D·∂²θ/∂z² − v·∂θ/∂z

    The new term on its own slides the whole shape down at speed v without changing it, like a conveyor belt. Together they spread and sink at once. In soil terms, v·θ is gravity's flux, K: here K = v·θ, a conductivity that grows in step with θ. Step 11 makes K more realistic.

    Explain it more slowly

    A shape sliding down unchanged at speed v is θ(z, t) = θ₀(z − v·t): whatever was at depth z₀ at the start is at z₀ + v·t later. Differentiate with the chain rule: ∂θ/∂t = −v·θ₀′ and ∂θ/∂z = θ₀′, so ∂θ/∂t = −v·∂θ/∂z. That is the drift term on its own.

    Which one wins? Drift moves the bump a distance v·t; diffusion spreads it by about √(2·D·t). The drift distance grows like t and the spread like √t, so over long times drift always wins. In soil, gravity is the drift: after a big watering it carries water down faster than spreading alone ever could.

    Notice: with v = 0 the bump spreads in place. With small D it slides almost unchanged. In the Richards equation the gravity term plays the drift, and its speed depends on how wet the soil is: fast in wet soil, nearly zero in dry soil. That is why wetting fronts stay sharp.

    Key idea: −v·∂θ/∂z shifts the profile down at speed v without changing its shape. With diffusion as well, the bump sinks and spreads.

    Check yourself: v = 2 cm/h and D = 0.5 cm²/h. After 2 h, how far has the bump moved, and roughly how much has it spread?

    Its center has moved v·t = 4 cm down. It has spread by about √(2·D·t) = √2 ≈ 1.4 cm.

    10b. Stack them: one pipe per boundary

    Put 10 cm of sand on top of 10 cm of clay, and slice the column into 8 layers as in step 8a. Each boundary between two layers is a pipe, and what crosses it in one time step is

    moved = r_b · (θ above − θ below)
    r_b = D_b · Δt / Δz²

    D_b is the D of the pipe at that boundary. Between two sand layers, it's sand's D. Between two clay layers, it's clay's. The boundary where sand meets clay needs one number too. Water leaving the bottom sand layer has to cross half a layer of sand and then half a layer of clay: two half-pipes in a row. Like two resistors in series, the slow half dominates:

    D_b = 2 ÷ (1/D_sand + 1/D_clay)
    = 2 ÷ (1/20 + 1/1.5) = 2.79 cm²/h

    That's close to clay's 1.5 and nowhere near sand's 20. Each layer is 2.5 cm and each step is 6 minutes.

    Depth runs down, top at the top. Each bar is one layer's θ. The orange links between layers are the pipes, drawn as thick as D_b, with the amount moved this step beside them.

    Notice: in the first two hours the water rushes through the sand pipes and the four sand layers nearly even out.     Key idea: In a layered pot each boundary has its own pipe. Where two materials meet, the pipe is two half-pipes in series, so the slower material dominates.

    Check yourself: D_sand = 20 and D_clay = 1.5. Is the sand–clay pipe closer to 20 or to 1.5?

    10c. Flux first, then its change

    Make the layers very thin and the bucket rule of 10b becomes a recipe with three steps. Each chart below is one step, with depth running down and every chart showing the same moment:

    1. slope ∂θ/∂z: how steeply θ changes with depth. Negative means θ falls as you go down.
    2. flux q = −D·∂θ/∂z: multiply by the D of whatever material is at that depth. Positive means water moving down.
    3. change ∂θ/∂t = −∂q/∂z: a thin layer gets wetter when more flows in its top than out its bottom.

    Put the three steps together and you get

    ∂θ/∂t = ∂/∂z [ D(z) · ∂θ/∂z ]

    D is used in step 2, before the last derivative is taken. That's all the brackets say. This is the sand-over-clay pot of 10b, sealed, just watered at the top.

    Explain it more slowly: why the flux can't jump

    Imagine a slice exactly at the boundary, so thin it holds no water at all. Whatever flows into its top in a second must flow out of its bottom in the same second; there is nowhere to keep the difference. So q just above equals q just below.

    Since q = −D·∂θ/∂z on each side, D_above·slope_above = D_below·slope_below. If D drops by a factor of 13, the slope must get 13 times steeper. That is the kink you see at 10 cm.

    1. slope ∂θ/∂z (per cm)

    2. flux q = −D·∂θ/∂z (cm/h)

    3. change ∂θ/∂t = −∂q/∂z (per hour)

    Notice: scrub to about 6 h and look at the 10 cm line. The slope jumps there: gentle in the sand, steep in the clay. The flux doesn't jump. It can't: if more arrived at the boundary than left it, the water would have to pile up in a layer of zero thickness. So D × slope is the same on both sides, and the slope in the clay is 13 times steeper to make up for its 13 times smaller D. The readouts check this at every moment.

    Key idea: Work out the flux first, with the local D, then take its change with depth. Across a boundary between materials the flux is continuous but the slope jumps.

    Check yourself: Just above a boundary D = 10 and the slope is −0.01 per cm. Below it D = 2. What is the slope just below?

    The flux must match: 10 × 0.01 = 2 × |slope|, so the slope below is −0.05 per cm, five times steeper.

    10d. Steady flow through layers: resistors in series

    Now keep the top wet (θ = 1, like a drip emitter that never stops) and let the bottom drain freely (θ = 0). The column starts dry. Water flows in at the top and out at the bottom, and eventually the two match: a steady state, as in step 9b.

    At steady state the flux is the same at every depth, so the slope in each layer is −q/D. Each layer behaves like a resistor with resistance = thickness ÷ D, and the total drop in θ from top to bottom is shared out like voltage across resistors in series.     Notice: early on, far more water enters at the top than leaves at the bottom, and the difference is what wets the column. The two lines then close in on the same value: steady state.     Check yourself: Two 10 cm layers with D = 10 and D = 1 have a total drop in θ of 0.5. What is the steady flux?

    10e. Why D goes inside: a conservation test

    Why not write D·∂²θ/∂z², with D outside? Inside each material D is constant, so the two forms agree there. They can only differ at the boundary. Here's a direct test: run the sealed, just-watered pot of 10c twice, once with each formula. The pot is sealed, so the total water has to stay fixed.

    A. flux first ∂θ/∂t = ∂/∂z [ D·∂θ/∂z ]
    B. D outside ∂θ/∂t = D·∂²θ/∂z²

    Notice: A keeps the total at exactly 5.2 cm. B loses about half the water within a day. Formula B quietly makes the slope match on both sides of the boundary instead of the flux. Then sand pushes water out at 20 × slope while clay takes it in at only 1.5 × slope, and the difference disappears. Formula A balances what flows in and out at every boundary, so nothing can disappear. That's why the Richards equation is written with K inside the brackets.

    Key idea: Only the form with D inside the derivative balances flux at every boundary, so only it conserves water where D jumps.

    Check yourself: Why can't formula B's mistake happen inside a single material?

    Inside one material D is the same on both sides of every face, so D·∂²θ/∂z² and ∂/∂z[D·∂θ/∂z] are identical. They only differ where D changes.

    10f. When D depends on wetness: the numbers

    In soil, the width of the pipe depends on how wet the soil is right there. In wet soil the pores form connected channels. In dry soil only thin films and isolated pockets remain. The simplest model with that behavior is a power law:

    D(θ) = (m + 1) · θ^m

    With m = 2, halving θ cuts D to a quarter, and a tenfold drop cuts it a hundredfold. Numbers this lopsided are unreadable on an ordinary axis, so both charts here use log scales: each gridline is ten times the one below it. On log axes a power law is a straight line, and its slope is m.

    Real soil is even more lopsided. This is D = K/C for the soil loaded in the lab (lab soil), from nearly saturated down to the wilting point. K is the pipe conductance and C the bucket capacity from Basics 4; their ratio plays the role of D. The other soil presets are dashed for comparison. Each curve runs from the wilting point on the left to nearly saturated on the right.

    Notice: Which D matters at a given boundary? The one for the wetness right there. The next step shows what that does to a wetting front.

    Check yourself: Why does a dry bucket two places ahead of the front stay exactly at its starting value?

    10h. The smooth version: nonlinear diffusion

    Shrink the buckets of 10g to zero thickness and the pipe rule becomes the flux-first equation of 10c, with the power-law D(θ) of 10f:

    ∂θ/∂t = ∂/∂z [ D(θ) · ∂θ/∂z ]
    D(θ) = D₀ · (m + 1) · θ^m

    With m = 0, D is constant and this is the heat equation. With m > 0, wet places conduct well and dry places barely conduct at all, which is how soil behaves.

    Back to the just-watered pot of 10a: 20 cm deep, sealed, top 4 cm at θ = 0.9. This time the soil below starts really dry, at θ = 0.02, because that's where the difference shows. With D₀ = 5 cm²/h, constant D (m = 0) behaves like a material between 10a's sand and clay.

    Notice: press play with m = 0. The water creeps down with a soft tail that reaches the bottom within about 12 hours, and the front-depth line slides smoothly down to 20 cm. Now raise m. The front turns into a cliff: wet behind it, bone dry ahead, and it advances at a finite speed that keeps slowing as the water above it spreads thinner. Dry soil ahead of the edge can't pass water along until it gets wet itself, just like the buckets of 10g. The total is the same in every case; only how it spreads changes. This is the sharp wetting front of Basics 5 and Lesson 5, in its simplest form.

    Key idea: A D(θ) that collapses in dry soil gives wetting fronts that are sharp and move at a finite speed. Constant D gives smooth tails that reach everywhere at once.

    Check yourself: Raising m makes the front sharper. What else happens to it?

    It slows down. The dry soil ahead barely conducts, so the front has to wet each layer before it can move on.

    11. Gravity with a K that depends on wetness

    In step 10 every part of the bump sank at the same speed v. In soil, gravity pulls water down through the pores at a rate K, and K depends on wetness (10f): wet soil drains fast, dry soil hardly at all. So the gravity flux is K(θ), not v·θ:

    ∂θ/∂t = −∂K(θ)/∂z + D·∂²θ/∂z²
    K(θ) = Ks · θ^p
    Ks = 8 cm/h, D = 0.4 cm²/h

    This is the Richards equation written in θ alone, minus roots: its flux is q = −D(θ)·∂θ/∂z + K(θ), diffusion plus gravity. In the soil chart of 10f, D = K/C. Here K(θ) = Ks·θ^p is a common soil model (Brooks–Corey), with p around 3 to 4 for real soils.

    How fast does one water content travel? Not K/θ, but the slope dK/dθ: the extra flow you get for a little extra water. With p = 1 that slope is the same everywhere, which is step 10's conveyor belt. With p > 1, wetter levels travel faster than drier ones.

    Explain it more slowly: why dK/dθ

    Add a tiny extra bit of water δθ at one depth. The flux there goes up by K′(θ)·δθ, so that extra bit gets carried along at speed K′(θ) = dK/dθ. It is the change in flux per change in water that moves a disturbance, not the flux itself.

    With K = 8·θ³ cm/h: at θ = 0.8, dK/dθ = 3 × 8 × 0.64 ≈ 15 cm/h; at θ = 0.05 it is 3 × 8 × 0.0025 = 0.06 cm/h. The wet back of the pulse moves about 250 times faster than the dry soil ahead.

    Wet levels can't pass through dry ones, so they pile up into a jump: the wetting front. The jump itself moves at the average speed across it, (K_wet − K_dry)/(θ_wet − θ_dry) = (4.10 − 0.001)/(0.8 − 0.05) ≈ 5.5 cm/h. Behind the front the opposite happens: the drier tail moves slower than the wet core, so it stretches out.

    Notice: with p = 1 every level sinks at the same speed, so the pulse slides down intact and only diffusion blurs it; in the depth-by-time map it's a straight diagonal stripe. Raise p. The wet core now outruns the dry soil ahead of it, so the leading edge piles up into a cliff that stays sharp as it falls: a gravity-driven wetting front. Behind it the opposite happens. As the back of the pulse dries, it slows down, so it stretches into a long, damp tail. In the map the stripe bends: a sharp lower edge and a smeared upper one.

    Key idea: With gravity, each water content travels at speed dK/dθ. When wetter means faster, the wet part catches up with the dry edge and the front sharpens itself.

    Check yourself: If K = Ks·θ (p = 1), what happens to the pulse as it falls?

    dK/dθ = Ks for every θ, so every part moves at the same speed and the pulse slides down unchanged, apart from diffusion blurring it: step 10 exactly.

    12. Two numbers for wetness: θ and h

    Until now θ ran from 0 to 1. Real θ is the fraction of the soil's volume that is water, and it never reaches either end. Even oven-dry soil keeps a little (θr, the residual content), and even saturated soil stays well below 1, because much of its volume is solid grains (θs, the saturated content, is around 0.4 for sand and loam and 0.7 for potting mix). The 0-to-1 version used in Part 2 is the effective saturation Se = (θ − θr)/(θs − θr), which Basics also uses. Diffusion doesn't care about the rescaling: every equation in Part 2 holds with real θ too.

    Real soil also needs a second number. h is the pressure head in cm: negative means suction, how hard the soil holds on to its water. The retention curve θ(h) links them, and every soil has its own (Basics 3 shows where it comes from).

    Explain it more slowly: an electrical picture

    Two questions with two answers. "How much water is in this soil?" is θ, what a scale or a capacitance probe measures. "How hard is it held?" is h, what a tensiometer measures and what roots feel.

    In circuit terms h is the voltage, θ is the stored charge, K is the conductance between nodes and C = dθ/dh is the capacitance. Current flows from high voltage to low, never "from more charge to less charge". Two capacitors of different size at the same charge sit at different voltages, and current flows between them. That is exactly the sand-and-loam pot.

    The twist in soil is that C and K both depend on h, which is what makes the Richards equation nonlinear.

    Water doesn't flow from wet to dry. It flows from weak suction to strong suction. To see the difference, put 10 cm of sandy soil on top of 10 cm of loam, both at exactly θ = 0.25, and switch gravity off for now (step 13 brings it back).

    Notice: at the start both halves hold exactly the same θ, yet water rushes from the sand into the loam. The sand sits at h ≈ −11 cm and the loam at −91 cm, so the loam pulls harder. Flow stops when h is the same everywhere, near −25 cm, and by then the loam holds more than twice as much water as the sand. At the boundary θ jumps but h doesn't, just as in 10c, where the slope jumped and the flux didn't. That's why the lab tracks h: it's smooth where θ isn't. The bucket capacity C = dθ/dh (Basics 4) converts between them: ∂θ/∂t = C(h)·∂h/∂t, and D = K/C from 10f is this conversion at work.

    Key idea: θ is how much water there is; h is how tightly it is held. Water flows toward more negative h, and h, not θ, is continuous across different soils.

    Check yourself: Sand and loam touch, both at θ = 0.3. Which way does water flow?

    Into the loam. At the same θ the loam holds its water much more tightly (more negative h), so it pulls water out of the sand.

    13. Gravity returns: total head

    With gravity on, water flows toward lower total head, suction plus height. With z measured down from the surface, height is −z, so

    H = h − z
    q = −K · ∂H/∂z = −K · (∂h/∂z − 1)

    That −1 is gravity, the drift of step 10 and 11. Flow stops when H is the same at every depth, which means h rises by 1 cm for every cm down. Here a 20 cm pot is soaked to saturation and then drains through a hole that holds the bottom at h = 0. Choose the soil:

    Explain it more slowly: the signs

    Height above the surface is −z, because z counts downward. Total head is pressure head plus height: H = h + (−z) = h − z.

    At rest nothing flows, so H is the same at every depth: H = H₀. Then h = H₀ + z. With h = 0 at the bottom (z = L), H₀ = −L, so h = z − L = −(L − z): minus the height above the bottom. In a 20 cm pot that is −20 cm at the top.

    Each depth's θ then follows from the retention curve at that h. Sand gives up most of its water by h = −20 cm; potting mix barely starts to. That is why the same pot drains so differently with different soils.

    Notice: on the head chart, H starts out tilted (water is flowing down) and swings upright as the pot drains. Once H is vertical, nothing moves, even though h is −20 cm at the top and 0 at the bottom. The θ profile is that straight h line passed through the retention curve. Sandy soil drains in under an hour: its upper half falls to about θ = 0.16 while the bottom few cm stay saturated, a perched water table. Loam drains less and slower, and potting mix ends up holding more than twice as much water as sand. This drained state is what the lab calls "full after draining", or container capacity, and it's why pot depth and shape matter (Lessons 6 and 7).

    Key idea: With gravity, water flows down the total head H = h − z. At rest H is the same everywhere, so h equals minus the height above the water table.

    Check yourself: A 30 cm pot sits in a saucer of water, so h = 0 at the bottom. At rest, what is h at the top?

    −30 cm. H is uniform, so h rises by 1 cm for every cm down, reaching 0 at the bottom 30 cm below.

    14. Roots: the sink term

    The last term, −S, removes water where the roots are. A simple model makes S the product of three things:

    S(z, t) = Tp(t) · r(z) · α(h)
    Tp(t) how much the plant wants to drink. Here 1 cm of water per day, drawn during daylight in a smooth hump, nothing at night.
    r(z) the share of roots at each depth: dense near the top, thinning out below.
    α(h) how easily roots can still pull water: 1 down to h = −300 cm, falling to 0 at −8000 cm, where the soil holds on too hard.

    15. Putting it together

    Here is the Richards equation again, with each piece traced back to a step in this primer:

    ∂θ/∂t = ∂/∂z [ K(h) (∂h/∂z − 1) ] − S
    ∂θ/∂t the rate of change at one fixed depth: a partial derivative in time (steps 1 and 7).
    ∂/∂z [ … ] the balance: what flows into a thin layer minus what flows out (step 8).
    K (∂h/∂z − 1) the flux with its sign flipped: water runs downhill in total head H = h − z, like q = −D ∂θ/∂z (steps 8 and 13). It sits inside ∂/∂z because the flux is worked out first, with the K that applies at each depth (steps 10a to 10e). Because K depends on h, the equation is nonlinear (steps 5 and 10f to 10h).
    θ and h two linked measures of wetness: θ is what's stored, h is what drives flow. The retention curve converts one to the other, and h stays smooth across layers where θ jumps (step 12).
    − 1 gravity inside that flux: a downward pull of strength K, the drift term (step 10), which sharpens wetting fronts when K depends on wetness (step 11).
    − S roots: a sink that depends on depth, time of day and how hard the soil holds its water (step 14), like the outflow in step 2.
    solving it chop depth into cells to get many coupled tank ODEs (steps 6 and 9), then step through time (step 3).

    Key idea: Every term of the Richards equation is now something you have built: storage change, flux balance, conductivity, gravity, and roots.

    Check yourself: Which term would you change to model a pot with a drip emitter instead of daily watering?

    None of the equation: the drip is a boundary condition, a steady flux q into the top. The equation describes the soil; the boundaries describe what you do to it.

    Reading the notation

    ∂θ/∂t
    How fast water content changes at one fixed depth. It is a partial derivative because θ depends on both depth z and time t.
    ∂q/∂z
    How fast the flux changes with depth. If it is positive, more water leaves a thin layer through its bottom than enters through its top, so the layer dries. That is why it enters the balance with a minus sign.
    ∂/∂z [ K ∂h/∂z ]
    Flux written in terms of h, then differentiated again: a second derivative in space. With constant K it becomes K ∂²h/∂z², the curvature of the profile, exactly as in the heat equation.
    Parabolic PDE
    The family the heat equation and Richards belong to. Disturbances spread and smooth out over time. You need one starting profile and a boundary condition at each end.

    1. One bucket: a first-order ODE

    Before any depth, treat the whole pot as one bucket that drains. Storage W changes only by outflow Q, and Q depends on how full the bucket is.

    dW/dt = −Q(W)
    linear: Q = W/τ
    nonlinear: Q = (W₀/τ)·(W/W₀)^p

    With p = 1 this is the first-order lag you know from controls, or an RC discharge: W = W₀e^(−t/τ). With p > 1 the outflow rate falls faster than the storage does, so draining starts at the same speed and then slows down.

    Notice: both buckets lose water at the same rate at t = 0. The nonlinear one is still holding water long after the linear one is empty, and on the log scale the linear bucket is a straight line while the nonlinear one bends.

    In the Richards equation

    A pot draining after a heavy watering behaves like the nonlinear bucket. At the base, flow is driven mainly by gravity, so the drainage rate is about K(θ), and K rises as a steep power of wetness.

    The linear bucket is also the simplest model you could drive a watering controller with: one state, one time constant. Everything the lab adds is what that model leaves out.

    2. Time steps: explicit vs implicit

    A computer can't integrate continuously, so it takes steps of size Δt. Take the linear bucket, dW/dt = −W/τ. Forward (explicit) Euler uses the outflow at the start of the step. Backward (implicit) Euler uses the outflow at the end, W[k+1] = W[k] − Δt·W[k+1]/τ, and solves for W[k+1]:

    forward: W[k+1] = (1 − Δt/τ)·W[k]
    backward: W[k+1] = W[k] / (1 + Δt/τ)

    Each step multiplies W by a growth factor G. If |G| > 1 the numbers blow up, whatever the physics says. This is the same stability question as a discrete-time pole sitting outside the unit circle.

    Notice: past Δt = τ, forward Euler overshoots below zero; past Δt = 2τ it oscillates and grows without bound. Backward Euler is never unstable. It just gets less accurate as Δt grows.

    In the Richards equation

    Every cell in the lab is a bucket with its own time constant, and wet, thin cells have very short ones.

    A system whose time constants span many orders of magnitude is called stiff, and stiff systems are why the lab uses implicit time steps.

    3. Capillary tubes: where θ(h) and K(h) come from

    Picture soil as a bundle of thin vertical tubes of different radii. A tube of radius r can hold water against a suction up to about 0.15/r (in cm, with r in cm). That is the height water rises in a capillary.

    Apply a suction. Every tube wider than the critical radius empties; every narrower tube stays full. Water held is the volume of the full tubes. Flow through a full tube goes as r⁴ (Poiseuille), so for the same volume of pore space, wide tubes carry far more flow.

    Notice: raise the suction slowly. The widest tubes empty first. They hold only a modest share of the water but carry most of the flow, so conductivity falls much faster than water content. When half the water is gone, K is typically down by a factor of 10 to 100.

    In the Richards equation

    The van Genuchten curve is a smooth fit to this picture. The median pore size plays the role of α (1/α is roughly the suction where the big pores start to drain), and the spread of pore sizes plays the role of n (a narrow spread is a large n and a steep curve). Mualem's K(h) formula is built on the same tube argument.

    4. Chain of buckets: how a PDE becomes ODEs

    Stack N buckets in a column, each joined to its neighbors by a pipe. The top bucket is joined to a reservoir held full (a ponded surface), and the bottom is sealed. Each bucket's water level plays the role of the pressure head h, and each bucket gets one ODE that only involves its neighbors:

    C·dh[i]/dt = G·(h[i−1] − 2h[i] + h[i+1])

    That is an RC ladder. Make the buckets thinner (Δz = L/N) with C ∝ Δz and G ∝ 1/Δz, and this becomes C·∂h/∂t = K·∂²h/∂z², or ∂h/∂t = D·∂²h/∂z² with D = K/C: the heat equation, written in the pressure head h of each bucket. The smooth line below is the same column cut into 200 buckets, close to the PDE's exact answer.

    Notice: with 3 buckets the profile is a staircase that lags the smooth curve; with 12 it nearly matches. The PDE is the limit of infinitely many infinitely thin buckets.

    In the Richards equation

    The lab's cells are exactly these buckets, and each time step solves this tridiagonal system. Two things differ: the pipe conductance at each face is K(h) and the bucket capacity is C(h), both changing as the soil wets and dries; and gravity adds a constant push down every pipe.

    5. Wetting fronts: heat equation to Richards

    Water is held at the top of a dry column. With constant diffusivity this is the heat equation, and the profile is a smooth error function. Then add the two ingredients that make Richards different, one at a time.

    ∂θ/∂t = ∂/∂z [ D(θ) ∂θ/∂z ] − ∂K(θ)/∂z
    D(θ) = Ds·e^(β(θ−1)), K(θ) = G·θ⁴

    Notice: with β = 0 the profiles are soft S-curves. As β grows, diffusivity is huge in wet soil and tiny in dry soil, so water floods through the wet zone and stalls at its edge: a sharp front. On the log-log chart, a slope of ½ means the front moves as √t (suction-driven, like heat), and a slope of 1 means steady speed (gravity-driven).

    In the Richards equation

    This is the θ-form of the equation, with diffusivity D = K/C. It is easy to read but breaks down at saturation, where C → 0 and D → ∞, and in layered soils, where θ jumps but h doesn't. That is why the lab solves for h instead, keeping θ in the storage term (the mixed form).

    D is scaled here so every setting soaks in about the same amount early on, and only the shape changes.

    6. Picard iteration: solving the implicit step

    Implicit Euler on the nonlinear bucket gives an equation with the unknown on both sides:

    W = W_old − Δt·k·W^p

    Picard iteration handles it the way the lab handles K(h): freeze the nonlinear coefficient at the latest guess, solve the now-linear equation, repeat.

    W[m+1] = W_old / (1 + Δt·k·W[m]^(p−1))

    The cobweb plot shows each pass. A step goes up to the curve (apply the update), then across to the diagonal (that answer becomes the next guess).

    Notice: the iteration settles only while |g′| < 1 at the answer. For this model that works out to (p − 1) × (share of the water removed in one step) < 1. Big steps through a strongly nonlinear region break it.

    In the Richards equation

    When Picard doesn't settle within 50 passes, the lab halves Δt and tries again, then lets the step grow back once things calm down. You can watch this as "Retried steps" in the solver stats. Newton's method converges faster near the answer but needs derivatives of K and θ; Picard with the mixed form is simpler and conserves mass.
